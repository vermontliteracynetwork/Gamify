# Why Gamify Works: The Research on Gamified Learning

Claudia's research brief, 2026-10-24. Covers the case **for** gamified
learning, the case **against** it, and how Gamify's design answers each
weakness. Every claim is tied to a published source (full references with
links at the end). Over half of the sources are from the last 10 years
(2016 to 2025); older sources are included where they are the landmark
studies everyone still cites.

---

## The short version

- **Gamified learning works, moderately.** Meta-analyses combining dozens
  of controlled studies find small-to-medium positive effects on learning,
  motivation and behavior (Sailer & Homner, 2020; Bai, Hew & Huang, 2020;
  Clark et al., 2016). A 2025 K-12 meta-analysis found a moderate effect
  on motivation (Kurnaz, 2025).
- **The learning comes from what the game makes students do, not from the
  points.** The strongest engine inside any quiz game is **retrieval
  practice**: pulling an answer from memory, again and again, spread over
  time. That is one of the best-proven techniques in all of learning
  science (Adesope et al., 2017; Agarwal et al., 2021; Cepeda et al., 2006).
- **The critics are right about specific designs, not about games.** The
  well-documented failures come from rewards that feel controlling,
  public leaderboards, novelty that wears off, and game layers bolted onto
  unchanged content (Deci et al., 1999; Hanus & Fox, 2015; Rodrigues et
  al., 2022; Mekler et al., 2017).
- **Gamify is designed around those exact failure points**: many game
  styles from one question set (novelty refresh), answering that powers
  play instead of points-for-points, no penalty for wrong answers, teacher
  control over competition, and accessibility built in.

## 1. What we mean by "gamified learning"

Two related ideas, and Gamify does both:

- **Gamification**: adding game design elements (points, levels, badges,
  leaderboards, story) to a non-game activity (Deterding et al., 2011).
- **Game-based learning**: the learning activity *is* a game (Clark et al.,
  2016).

Gamify's question sets are the learning activity; the games around them
are the motivation and the repetition engine. Research on both bodies of
work applies.

## 2. The case FOR gamified learning

### 2.1 The big-picture evidence (meta-analyses)

A meta-analysis pools many controlled studies to find the average effect.
Effect sizes (Hedges' g) of about 0.2 are small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
For context, the average yearly learning gain in many school studies is in
the 0.3 to 0.5 range, so effects at this size are educationally real.

| Study | What it pooled | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Sailer & Homner (2020)**, *Educational Psychology Review* | 40 experiments from 38 publications | Positive effects on cognitive learning (g = 0.49), motivation (g = 0.36) and behavior (g = 0.25). The learning effect held up in the most rigorous studies. Game fiction (story) and social interaction made behavioral effects stronger. |
| **Bai, Hew & Huang (2020)**, *Educational Research Review* | 30 interventions from 24 studies, plus 32 qualitative studies | Medium overall effect on learning (g = 0.504). Students liked that gamification built enthusiasm, gave feedback, recognized effort and helped goal setting. |
| **Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016)**, *Review of Educational Research* | K-16 digital game studies | Games beat non-game instruction (effect about 0.33), and **better-designed games beat basic games** by about 0.34. Design matters as much as the medium. |
| **Kurnaz (2025)**, *Psychology in the Schools* | K-12 experimental studies | Moderate effect on motivation (g = 0.654), driven by clear goals, timely feedback and difficulty matched to skill, not by the number of game features. |
| **Wang & Tahir (2020)**, *Computers & Education* | 93 studies of Kahoot!, the closest existing product to a live quiz game | Positive effects on learning performance, classroom dynamics, student and teacher attitudes, and student anxiety, though some studies found little or no effect. |

### 2.2 Repetition: why quiz games are a delivery system for retrieval practice

This is Gamify's strongest scientific foundation, and it does not depend
on whether students like the games (though they do).

- **Retrieval practice (the testing effect).** Answering a question from
  memory strengthens learning more than re-reading or re-studying the same
  material. A meta-analysis of practice testing found it beat restudying
  and every other comparison condition (Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan,
  2017). A systematic review of 50 experiments **in real classrooms**
  (5,000+ students) found retrieval practice consistently helped, with
  medium-to-large effects across subjects, grade levels and question
  formats (Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt, 2021).
- **Spaced practice.** Practice spread out over time beats the same amount
  crammed together. A synthesis of 317 experiments found longer gaps
  between practice sessions produce longer-lasting memory (Cepeda et al.,
  2006).
- **Interleaving.** Mixing different kinds of problems in one session
  improves later test performance compared with practicing one kind at a
  time (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007).
- **Feedback.** Feedback is one of the most powerful influences on
  learning, especially when it is about the task and comes quickly
  (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). Feedback after multiple-choice practice
  increases the benefit of testing and reduces the risk of students
  remembering wrong options (Butler & Roediger, 2008).

**The problem games solve:** retrieval practice works, but repeated
drilling is boring, and bored students stop practicing. The research
problem has never been "does practice work," it is "how do you get a
7-year-old to do 40 more reps willingly." A game wraps each repetition in
something the student wants to do next.

**How Gamify delivers it:**
- Every game is fueled by the same teacher question set, so a student
  answering 60 questions across Peg Drop, Bubble Pop and Match Up has done
  60 retrieval reps on the *same content*, without it feeling like the
  same worksheet.
- The question picker mixes sets and avoids immediate repeats (built-in
  interleaving and spacing within a session); a teacher can star a focus
  set to concentrate practice where it is needed.
- Games return day after day with the same content in a new form, which
  is spaced practice by design.

### 2.3 The dopamine question: what the brain science actually says

- Dopamine is released during video game play. A brain-imaging study found
  dopamine release in the striatum (a reward area) while people played a
  goal-directed game, and the amount tracked how well they were doing
  (Koepp et al., 1998).
- Dopamine is less about pleasure and more about **anticipation and
  learning from surprise**: it fires most when an outcome is better than
  expected (Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997). That is why "what will I
  get?" moments in games are so engaging.
- **Curiosity boosts memory through the same circuit.** When people were
  curious about an answer, their reward circuit and hippocampus (the
  memory center) became more active and connected, and they remembered
  both the answer **and unrelated material shown during that curious
  state** better, even a day later (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014).
- **Uncertain rewards increase engagement with learning.** In a classroom
  math quiz, 10-11 year olds given the choice preferred a chance-based
  reward over a guaranteed point, and that preference grew during the
  quiz; uncertain reward also predicted recall in a learning game
  (Howard-Jones & Demetriou, 2009; Howard-Jones et al., 2011).

**How Gamify uses it, carefully:**
- The question break creates anticipation: answer, then see what the ball
  does, what card flips, what the shot hits. The right answer is the
  trigger for the exciting moment, which links the reward to the learning
  act itself.
- Surprise elements (Team Board cards, Peg Drop buckets) use chance for the
  *game outcome*, never for whether learning counts: a right answer always
  counts.
- **Honesty guardrail:** "dopamine" is a popular buzzword and one of the
  most common sources of "neuromyths" in education (Howard-Jones, 2014).
  Gamify's marketing should say "games build anticipation and curiosity,
  which research links to better memory," not "Gamify releases dopamine."

### 2.4 Variety: why many game styles matter

- **Self-Determination Theory.** People are most motivated when three
  needs are met: **competence** (I'm getting better), **autonomy** (I have
  a choice) and **relatedness** (I'm connected to others) (Ryan & Deci,
  2000). Video games are compelling largely because they satisfy these
  needs (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski, 2006).
- **Different game elements meet different needs.** Badges, leaderboards
  and progress graphs raised feelings of competence; avatars, story and
  teammates raised feelings of social relatedness (Sailer et al., 2017).
  No single game design covers every need.
- **Story and social play add effect.** Game fiction and social
  interaction strengthened gamification's effects on behavior (Sailer &
  Homner, 2020).
- **Design quality, not just "being a game."** Augmented game designs
  outperformed basic versions (Clark et al., 2016).

**How Gamify's four modes and many games map onto this:**

| Need | Where Gamify meets it |
|---|---|
| Competence | Visible progress inside each game, assignment progress, "2 of 3" break pill, support levels that adjust to the student |
| Autonomy | Students choose the game (or the teacher offers a choice board); every game uses the same question screen, so choice never costs learning |
| Relatedness | Share (team play on one screen), Class Live (whole class together), Versus (play a friend), class-designed themes |
| Different kinds of players | Arcade action, puzzle, strategy (chess, Four in a Row), building/tycoon, calm activities (match, sort, flashcards) |

## 3. The case AGAINST gamified learning (taken seriously)

### 3.1 Rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation (the overjustification effect)
A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected, tangible rewards
for doing, finishing or performing a task reduced people's free-choice
interest in that task afterward (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999). Positive
feedback, by contrast, *increased* intrinsic motivation. The critique:
"if you pay kids in points to learn, they learn for points, and stop
when the points stop."

### 3.2 Gamification can backfire in real classrooms
In a semester-long study, students in a gamified course (required badges,
a leaderboard) ended with **lower** intrinsic motivation, lower
satisfaction and lower final exam scores than students in the same course
without it; the authors pointed to social comparison and loss of autonomy
(Hanus & Fox, 2015). Reviews of gamification's negative effects list
loss of performance, undesired behavior (gaming the system), indifference
and effects that decline over time (Toda, Valle & Isotani, 2018).

### 3.3 Points and leaderboards can raise quantity, not quality or love of learning
Points, levels and leaderboards made people do *more* of a task, but did
not raise intrinsic motivation or feelings of competence; they worked as
extrinsic incentives only (Mekler et al., 2017).

### 3.4 The novelty effect
Gamification's boost dropped after about four weeks as the novelty wore
off (Rodrigues et al., 2022). Critics argue many short studies measure
excitement about something new, not lasting learning.

### 3.5 Competition, time pressure and anxiety
Students who disliked gamification cited anxiety and jealousy (Bai, Hew &
Huang, 2020). In Kahoot! research, students reported stressful time
pressure and questions that were hard to read on a projected screen
(Wang & Tahir, 2020). Lower-performing students see themselves at the
bottom of a public ranking, every round.

### 3.6 The evidence itself is uneven
Effects vary widely between studies, and the motivational and behavioral
effects are less stable than the learning effect (Sailer & Homner, 2020).
Effects depend on sample size and duration (Bai, Hew & Huang, 2020). Much
research is in higher education rather than K-12, and retrieval-practice
classroom research has very little from primary schools (Agarwal et al.,
2021). The research on students with ADHD, autism and learning
disabilities is promising but built on small, short studies.

### 3.7 "Chocolate-covered broccoli" and guessing to get back to the game
When the game is fun and the learning is a toll gate in front of it,
students rush or guess through questions to get back to play. And game
visuals that have nothing to do with the learning can compete for limited
attention. Better game design, not just "a game," is what produces gains
(Clark et al., 2016).

## 4. Where Gamify answers the counter-arguments

| Counter-argument | Why it usually happens | Gamify's design answer | Evidence behind the answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rewards kill intrinsic motivation (3.1) | Tangible, expected rewards that feel controlling | Rewards are **game moves, not prizes**: a right answer powers the game you're already playing. No real money, no prizes, no paid rewards. Feedback is positive and about the task. | Positive feedback raises intrinsic motivation (Deci et al., 1999); task-focused feedback works best (Hattie & Timperley, 2007) |
| Gamified courses backfired (3.2) | Mandatory badges, public leaderboards, no autonomy | Students choose games; teacher can **hide rankings** or use a **class goal bar**; badges are never required to complete work | Social comparison and autonomy loss were the suspected causes (Hanus & Fox, 2015); autonomy is a core motivation need (Ryan & Deci, 2000) |
| More quantity, not better learning (3.3) | Points reward doing more, not thinking | Every rep is **retrieval practice** on the teacher's own content; the learning act itself is the point-earner, so quantity *is* quality here | Retrieval practice reliably improves learning in classrooms (Agarwal et al., 2021; Adesope et al., 2017) |
| Novelty wears off (3.4) | One game, one mechanic, same thing every day | **Dozens of games and four modes from one question set**, plus themes that change the look across all games. New is always one tap away; content stays the same | Effects recovered with familiarization at 6-10 weeks (Rodrigues et al., 2022); varied elements meet different needs (Sailer et al., 2017) |
| Competition and time pressure cause anxiety (3.5) | Public, timed, ranked play every round | Wrong answers **never cost anything**; 3-second read-first instead of a race clock; timers optional; leaderboards optional; Versus pays both players for right answers; Share uses **team** scores; calm mode | Students cited anxiety and jealousy (Bai et al., 2020) and time pressure (Wang & Tahir, 2020) as the main dislikes |
| Students guess to get back to the game (3.7) | Fast wrong answers have no cost and the game resumes | One try per question; a wrong answer brings a **new** question, not the same one to guess again; a **guessing check** pauses rapid wrong taps with a calm break | Retrieval only helps when students actually retrieve |
| Uneven evidence, little for young or neurodivergent learners (3.6) | Most studies are college-level, short and small | Gamify is built and tested for K-3 readers and students with high support needs first (Three-Lens standard, read-aloud, reading comfort, calm mode) and will **collect its own outcome data** (section 5) | UDL 3.0 and the accessibility research in `docs/accessibility-research.md` |

### Where Gamify still has to improve (honest gaps)
1. **Show the right answer at some point.** The Dashboard engine hides the
   correct answer after a miss so students can't just copy it. But
   research says corrective feedback is what makes multiple-choice
   practice stick and prevents students from learning wrong options
   (Butler & Roediger, 2008). Proposal: keep "no reveal mid-game," but add
   an end-of-game **"Let's look back"** review showing each missed question
   with the right answer, read aloud.
2. **Spacing across days.** Gamify spaces within a session; it should also
   bring back missed and older questions on later days (a simple "review
   pile"), which is where spacing pays off most (Cepeda et al., 2006).
3. **Don't overclaim.** Marketing must stay inside what the research
   supports: no "dopamine" promises, no "proven to raise test scores"
   until Gamify has its own data.

## 5. How Gamify proves its own success

Schools buying software increasingly ask for evidence. The federal ESSA
evidence tiers run from Tier IV ("demonstrates a rationale," a
research-based logic model, which this document supports) up to Tier I
(strong, from a randomized study). Plan:

1. **Logic model (now, Tier IV):** question sets + game modes → more
   voluntary retrieval reps, spaced over days, with feedback → better
   retention of the taught content. This document is the research base.
2. **Built-in measures (Phase 1-2):** per-question accuracy over time,
   accuracy on the same questions a week later (retention), number of reps
   per student per week, how often students choose to keep playing after
   an assignment is done (voluntary engagement), guessing-check rate.
3. **Pilot (Phase 2):** a few classrooms, compare unit quiz scores on
   content practiced in Gamify vs. content taught the usual way, plus a
   short teacher and student survey. Correlational results support Tier
   III ("promising evidence").
4. **Later:** partner with a university for a comparison study (Tier II or
   I) once there are paying schools.
