# Junior Designers -- co-designing Gamify with 5th graders

Companion doc to the published plan: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/24b58192-de08-4fd3-b3f6-99984c3766a4

The classroom (5th grade, including neurodivergent students with high
support needs) will help design Gamify's features -- but the teacher drives
every tool. Students never operate software directly; they create ideas,
the teacher captures them. That constraint shapes everything below.

Every task is built to practice three named things, on purpose -- not as a
side effect of "doing design":

1. **Descriptive language**
2. **Game design thinking**
3. **Following steps in order**

## The three things this practices

| Pillar | How |
|---|---|
| Descriptive language | Every task ends with the student describing their idea in words -- color words, size words, feeling words. Their words become real input, not a worksheet exercise. |
| Game design thinking | Tasks aren't only "what color" -- they also ask what happens when you win, what makes something hard or easy, what makes a game fun to keep playing. |
| Following steps in order | The same five steps run every single time, taught to the class by name. An idea's whole journey to the real game is a sequence they can say out loud. |

## How a student creates an idea

Three ways in, matched to how a student communicates best -- not a skill
ranking. The teacher runs all the tools; a student never has to operate
software to take part.

- **Draw it** -- sketches on paper or a tablet, in whatever detail they're able to give.
- **Pick it** -- shown 2-3 options side by side, indicates a favorite: pointing, an AAC device, eye gaze, or a verbal yes/no as the teacher points to each.
- **Make it** -- paper, crayons, stickers, blocks, playdough, whatever they already reach for. The teacher photographs the result.

**Then, every time: Tell it.** "Tell me about it" -- whatever words a
student has, even one word ("big," "scary," "shiny"). The teacher writes
down exactly what they say. This line goes to Claude along with the
drawing, the pick, or the photo -- the description is part of the real
design spec, not a caption on it.

## Our Steps (taught to the class, by name)

1. **We get a question.** The teacher picks one small, real question tied to an actual feature, scoped to one sitting.
2. **We make our idea.** Draw it, Pick it, or Make it, then Tell it, every time.
3. **We put it in the folder.** Everything -- drawings, photos, picks, descriptions -- into one shared Drive folder as real files.
4. **Claude builds it.** Claude reviews the whole batch and turns the common thread into a real spec and real code.
5. **We play it!** Screenshot or live link, shown back to the room: that's the idea they made, really in the game. Never skip this one -- it's the step that makes steps 1-4 mean anything.

## Question bank, split on purpose

Alternate between these two columns so "design" doesn't quietly become
just "pick a color" -- game-design questions are just as much the point as
visual ones.

| Looks | Plays |
|---|---|
| What color should the winning trophy be? | What should happen when you get an answer wrong? |
| What should the game's mascot look like? | How many rounds feels like enough -- not too short, not too long? |
| What should happen on the screen when you win? | What would make this game harder? What would make it easier? |

## Why these three things

- **Descriptive language** -- "Tell it" is real, functional description practice: their exact words go into the actual product, so it isn't practice for practice's sake.
- **Game design thinking** -- the "Plays" column asks students to reason about rules, difficulty, and fun, a different, higher-order skill than picking a favorite color.
- **Following steps in order** -- "Our Steps" is a real five-step sequence a student can learn to say and predict: procedural/sequencing practice with a visible, motivating payoff at the end.
- **Cause and effect** (bonus) -- seeing your own choice show up in a game other kids play is a concrete result a student can point to and say "I did that."

## Tools -- the teacher operates all of them

- **Google Drive** -- the one shared folder. Everything from every task lands here: scans of drawings, photos of physical work, a note on which option got picked, and the written-down description. If a Drive connector gets linked to this project, Claude can read straight from that folder instead of exporting and uploading each item by hand.
- **Google Slides** -- optional, teacher-only. Handy for tidying a batch of student ideas into one clean page before sending it over; never something a student needs to open.
- **Figma** -- not needed here. Genuinely useful when a designer hands off precise specs to an engineer, but that handoff role doesn't exist in this loop; Drive alone already covers collection.

## Before the first task goes out

These are minors, some with disability status, and their work is feeding
an AI-assisted development process -- worth handling deliberately, not by
default.

- **Photograph the work, not the student.** No faces in the shared folder.
- Use first names or student IDs internally, never in anything uploaded or shared with Claude.
- Check with the school on whatever policy already governs student work used in outside tools -- this should follow that process, not sit outside it.
